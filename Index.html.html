03

Methodology

How every figure on this page is derived
STEP 1

Bed density

Calculated as (bed supply ÷ population) × 1,000 for every region and year, giving beds available per 1,000 residents — the standard WHO/OECD comparability metric.

STEP 2

Gap vs. global average

Required beds are modelled at the global reference of 2.9 beds / 1,000. Gap = (population ÷ 1,000 × 2.9) − actual beds. A positive figure is a shortfall; negative is a surplus above the global norm.

STEP 3 & 4

Gap vs. domestic standard

Abu Dhabi and Dubai are also benchmarked against the UAE's own national bed density for that year; Riyadh and Jeddah against KSA's national density. This isolates intra-country imbalance separately from the global comparison.

STEP 5

Prior-year comparison

UAE-side figures compare 2024 (current) against 2023 (prior); KSA-side figures compare 2022 (current) against 2021 (prior) — reflecting the latest full-year datasets published by each country's statistics authority at the time of this analysis.

Sources. UAE & Abu Dhabi & Dubai population: Federal Competitiveness & Statistics Centre (FCSC), Statistics Centre – Abu Dhabi (SCAD), and Dubai Statistics Center (DSC) — 2023 figures reconstructed from each authority's published year-on-year growth rates where a disaggregated prior-year release was not independently re-verifiable. KSA, Riyadh & Jeddah population: General Authority for Statistics (GASTAT), including the 2022 population estimates release; 2021 figures derived from GASTAT's published national growth rate (mid-2021 population of ~34.1M nationally, with city-level 2021 estimates back-calculated from GASTAT/UN-linked metro growth rates). Bed supply: Dubai Health Authority (DHA) and Department of Health – Abu Dhabi (DOH) statistical yearbooks for UAE emirates; Saudi Ministry of Health (MOH) statistical yearbook for KSA, Riyadh and Jeddah. Note: 2023 UAE-level and 2021 KSA-level bed counts are estimated using each market's typical 2–3% y-o-y licensed-capacity growth rate, since fully disaggregated prior-year emirate/city bed breakdowns are not always published in a single consolidated table — treat prior-year bed figures as indicative until cross-checked against the primary DHA/DOH/MOH yearbook tables.

Investment & development signals

Recent, publicly reported activity by market

Dubai

Density near global average
  • Dubai Healthcare City Authority committed AED 1.3 billion toward new clinical capacity and roughly 400 additional beds plus 250 medical roles, signalling continued public-private capacity build-out.
  • Home-based healthcare is scaling fast — around 65% of patients aged 60+ across Dubai/Abu Dhabi now prefer home care over clinic visits, opening a lower-capex alternative to inpatient beds.
  • Medical tourism and digital insurance are both compounding (double-digit growth), widening the addressable market beyond resident population alone.
  • AI-driven diagnostics and "smart hospital" pilots are becoming a differentiator for new private entrants.
Read: capacity gap is moderate but demand-side growth (tourism, insurance, aging residents) argues for targeted specialty and step-down/home-care investment rather than pure bed-count expansion.

Abu Dhabi

Above UAE average density
  • Arada is deploying $545M to build an 800-bed private network, doubling Reem Hospital to 200 beds and adding new facilities across Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Sharjah.
  • Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City (SEHA / Mayo Clinic partnership) is expanding cardiology, neurology and critical-care capacity — a specialty rather than volume play.
  • Emirate-level sovereign wealth capital continues to back both domestic and outbound healthcare investment.
Read: Abu Dhabi already sits above the UAE national average, so the opportunity skews toward tertiary/quaternary specialty capacity and export of clinical expertise, not general bed growth.

Riyadh

Largest absolute shortfall
  • Riyadh anchors roughly 180 private hospitals and ~22,000 private beds nationally, concentrated across Riyadh, Jeddah and the Eastern Province under Vision 2030 privatization.
  • The "Regional Headquarters" program has pulled 400+ global companies into the city, accelerating expat-driven population and insured-patient growth.
  • King Abdullah Financial District and Riyadh Metro expansion are reshaping where new facilities should be sited relative to population density shifts.
  • NUPCO framework agreements remain the primary channel for medical device and supply-chain entrants targeting public procurement.
Read: Riyadh carries the largest absolute global-standard bed gap in this dataset — the strongest case in the set for new general-acute capacity, ideally paired with the corridors opening around the new financial and metro districts.

Jeddah

Steady mid-tier gap
  • The $20B Jeddah Central Project is transforming the waterfront into a tourism and residential hub, adding sustained population and visitor-driven healthcare demand.
  • King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Centre is expanding with two new patient towers and a dedicated research/academic centre — a signal for specialty and academic medicine investment.
  • As the principal gateway for Umrah/Hajj pilgrims (18M+ annually), Jeddah carries structurally elevated seasonal demand that resident-population bed ratios understate.
Read: baseline resident bed gap is moderate, but pilgrim-season surge capacity and specialty/academic medicine are the more differentiated opportunities.
05

Analyst take

How to read this dashboard for decision-making

Bed density alone can be misleading — a region can sit near the global average while still carrying real capacity strain if its population is growing faster than licensed beds, or if a large share of demand is transient (tourism, pilgrimage, medical travel). The two-lens approach used here — global benchmark and domestic standard — is designed to separate two different investment theses:

  1. Global-standard gap answers "how far is this market from an internationally normal level of care access" — useful for greenfield general-hospital investment and government capacity planning.
  2. Domestic-standard gap answers "is this city under- or over-served relative to its own country" — useful for identifying where private operators can win share from a national peer network, or where a city is already saturated and specialty/niche capacity is the smarter play.

On the current dataset: Riyadh shows the largest absolute shortfall against the global benchmark and a real (if smaller) shortfall against KSA's own average — the clearest combined signal for new general-acute capacity. Jeddah trails at a similar density to Riyadh but with pilgrim-driven seasonal surge as an added consideration. Dubai sits slightly below the UAE national average, a modest opening for targeted capacity, while Abu Dhabi already runs above the UAE average — its opportunity is specialty and tertiary care rather than bed count.
